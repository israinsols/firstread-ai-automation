In the UK, the clocks change twice a year. They go forward in spring and back in autumn. Remembering the rule is useful, but check the date for the year you mean before making plans: the exact calendar date changes each year.

The simple rule on [GOV.UK's official clock-change page](https://www.gov.uk/when-do-the-clocks-change) is that clocks go forward by one hour at **1am on the last Sunday in March**, and back by one hour at **2am on the last Sunday in October**. The official page lists the actual dates for the current and upcoming years.

## When do clocks go forward?

On the last Sunday in March, UK clocks move forward one hour at 1am. The period when clocks are one hour ahead is called **British Summer Time (BST)**. GOV.UK notes that this means more daylight in the evening and less in the morning.

Because the last Sunday falls on a different date each year, do not assume the change is always on the same numbered day. If you are organising a trip, work shift, appointment or online event, check the official calendar for the specific year.

## When do clocks go back?

On the last Sunday in October, clocks move back one hour at 2am. The UK returns to **Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)**, according to GOV.UK.

If you are working overnight or planning transport, the change can affect how times appear. Follow your employer's or transport provider's instructions and use the time printed on the current ticket or official timetable. This guide is not a substitute for checking a live service.

## Do phones and computers change automatically?

Many internet-connected devices update their time automatically when the relevant setting is enabled, but do not assume every clock or device does. Check the date and time settings on each device you rely on, especially if it is offline or set to a different time zone.

Some clocks, appliances and vehicle displays may need to be updated manually. If an alarm or appointment matters, check the displayed time before you go to bed and again after the change. Avoid changing one device's settings without checking whether it also affects calendar or reminder times.

Online calendars and video meetings can display times according to the organiser's time zone. Before joining an event, check the invitation's date, time and time zone. If you are arranging a meeting with someone elsewhere, state the time zone rather than assuming both calendars show the same local time.

## How the clock change can affect plans

**Appointments:** Look at the appointment message or service directly. If it is important and the displayed time seems unclear, confirm it with the organiser.

**Travel:** Check the latest information from the train, coach, airline or other operator. A search result or old screenshot may not reflect a current timetable.

**Work shifts:** Follow your employer's rota and ask a manager if an overnight shift crosses the change and the recorded hours are unclear.

**Alarms:** Check that your phone uses automatic date and time settings if that is what you expect. Consider setting a backup alarm on a separate device for an important event.

**Household clocks:** Update clocks that do not adjust automatically, such as a microwave or some car displays. You do not need to change them all immediately, but do check them before relying on one.

## What if you work overnight?

If your shift crosses the clock change, the elapsed time and the clock time may not line up in the usual way. In spring, the hour between 1am and 2am is skipped; in autumn, the hour between 1am and 2am occurs twice. Ask your employer how the rota, recorded hours and handover should be handled rather than trying to infer pay or working-time rules from a general guide.

The same extra check is useful for childcare handovers, timed medication, appointments and other arrangements where precision matters. Write down the date, local time and time zone, then confirm the instruction with the person or organisation responsible. If a device displays an unexpected time, compare it with a reliable time source before acting.

## A quick way to remember the direction

In spring, clocks **go forward** one hour and the UK moves into BST. In autumn, clocks **go back** one hour and the UK returns to GMT. That memory aid gives you the direction, but the official GOV.UK page is the place to confirm the exact date for a particular year.

If you use an online calendar, add the event in the organiser's stated time zone where possible. If a meeting invitation is ambiguous, ask for clarification instead of guessing. For transport, use the operator's own live timetable rather than relying on a general article.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When do clocks go forward in the UK?

GOV.UK says the clocks go forward by one hour at 1am on the last Sunday in March. Check its official page for the date in the year you need.

### When do clocks go back in the UK?

GOV.UK says the clocks go back by one hour at 2am on the last Sunday in October. The UK then returns to GMT.

### Does BST mean the same thing as GMT?

No. GOV.UK describes BST as the period when clocks are one hour ahead. When clocks go back in autumn, the UK returns to GMT.

### Will every clock change automatically?

No. Many connected devices can update automatically, but some appliances and clocks may need a manual change. Check each device before relying on its displayed time.

## Check the date before making plans

The rule helps you remember which way the clock moves, but the date itself changes annually. Before arranging travel, shifts or appointments, confirm the relevant year's dates on [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/when-do-the-clocks-change) and check the organiser's or provider's current schedule.
