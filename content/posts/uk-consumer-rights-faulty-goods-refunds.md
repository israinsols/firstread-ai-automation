Bought a product that does not work, does not match its description or was damaged when it arrived? Your options can depend on what you bought, where you bought it, how long ago you bought it and what has gone wrong. A shop's returns policy is not the only information that may matter, but neither does every change of mind guarantee a refund.

This guide summarises general UK guidance and is not legal advice for an individual dispute. Check the official [GOV.UK guidance on accepting returns and giving refunds](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds) and the [consumer-rights contact page](https://www.gov.uk/consumer-protection-rights) for details relevant to your circumstances.

## Faulty goods and a change of mind are different

GOV.UK says a business must offer a full refund if an item is faulty, not as described or does not do what it is supposed to do. Separate rules can apply if you simply change your mind, and time limits or exceptions may affect what you can ask for.

When contacting a seller, describe the issue in plain language. Include the purchase date, order number, what went wrong and what outcome you are asking about. Keep a copy of the receipt, order confirmation and relevant messages. If a product fault is visible, photos or a short video may help explain it, but do not make a repair attempt if that could be unsafe or affect your options.

## What if you bought the item in a shop?

GOV.UK says you do not have to refund a customer just because they no longer want an item, such as an unwanted colour or size, unless they bought it without seeing it. Different rights may apply if the item is faulty or was not as described.

Check the receipt and store policy, but do not assume that a “no refunds” sign settles a complaint about a faulty product. GOV.UK says it is illegal to restrict or take away customers' rights or mislead them about those rights. Explain the problem to the retailer and ask what remedy it offers under the relevant rules.

## What if you ordered online, by phone or by post?

For many online, mail or telephone orders, the rules allow customers to cancel within a limited time even if an item is not faulty. GOV.UK says customers must tell the seller within 14 days of receiving the item that they want to cancel, then have another 14 days to return it. It also says the refund is due within 14 days of the seller receiving the item back.

The rules have exceptions. The GOV.UK page lists examples such as personalised or custom-made items, perishable goods, some opened software and sealed goods that cannot be returned for health or hygiene reasons after opening. Read the current full guidance rather than assuming every purchase has the same cancellation period.

GOV.UK says the seller must refund standard delivery costs when a qualifying online order is cancelled. If you paid extra for a more expensive delivery method, the seller only needs to refund the standard delivery cost. The product and situation still matter, so check the details on the official page.

## Repairs, replacements and older faults

A refund is not the only possible outcome. Depending on the purchase and the circumstances, a repair or replacement may be relevant. GOV.UK says that if a customer returns an item within six months and asks for a repair or replacement, the seller must provide one unless it can prove the item was not faulty when bought.

The same page states that customers can have up to six years to make a claim for an item they have bought, or five years in Scotland. These are not blanket guarantees that every product can be returned for six years: the nature of the issue, evidence, purchase details and applicable law matter. Read the official guidance and seek advice for your situation.

Do not rely only on a manufacturer's warranty to decide whether to contact the shop that sold the item. GOV.UK says consumer rights to a repair or replacement can exist independently of a warranty or guarantee. Ask the seller to explain its position in writing.

## A sensible order of steps

1. **Check the purchase record.** Find the receipt or order confirmation, the seller's name and the purchase and delivery dates.
2. **Describe the problem.** Explain whether the product is faulty, different from its description, or simply no longer wanted.
3. **Contact the seller.** Use its official customer-service details and keep a copy of your complaint.
4. **Ask what remedy is available.** Depending on the facts, this could involve a refund, repair or replacement.
5. **Check the official guidance.** Use the relevant GOV.UK page and note any exceptions or time limits that may apply.
6. **Get advice if you are stuck.** The contact service depends on where in the UK you live.

Trading Standards may investigate businesses that break the law, but that is different from obtaining a personal refund. [Citizens Advice explains its role](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/) and how to report certain business practices.

## Where to get consumer advice in the UK

The GOV.UK consumer-rights page lists different contacts: Citizens Advice for England and Wales, Advice Direct Scotland for Scotland, and Consumerline for Northern Ireland. Choose the service that applies where you live and follow its current instructions.

For a complaint involving a seller in another country, a digital service, a financial product or a special contract, ordinary goods-return advice may not be enough. Identify what you bought and who sold it before seeking help.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a UK shop refuse a refund for a faulty item?

GOV.UK says a full refund must be offered if an item is faulty, not as described or does not do what it is supposed to do. The best next step depends on the purchase details and the issue.

### Can I return an online purchase if I changed my mind?

Many online orders have a limited cancellation period, but there are exceptions. Check the current GOV.UK rules for the product and purchase type before assuming that cancellation applies.

### What should I do if a shop will not help?

Keep your proof of purchase and correspondence, explain the issue in writing, and seek advice from the consumer service for your UK nation. Citizens Advice notes that Trading Standards investigations and an individual refund are different things.

### Is a manufacturer's warranty the same as consumer rights?

No. GOV.UK says rights to a repair or replacement may exist regardless of whether a warranty or guarantee is in place. Check the official guidance for your specific circumstances.

## Check the rules that fit your purchase

Consumer problems can have different answers depending on what was bought, when it was bought and the seller's response. Start with the official GOV.UK [returns and refunds guidance](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds), keep your records and contact the consumer advice service for your part of the UK if you need help. This article is general information, not a decision about your claim.
