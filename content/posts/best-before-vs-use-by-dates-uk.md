UK food labels often show a “use by” or “best before” date. The words look similar, but they serve different purposes. Knowing which one is on a packet can help you follow the right safety advice and avoid throwing away food solely because a quality date has passed.

The short version is: **use-by dates are about safety; best-before dates are about quality**. The UK government says most prepacked food must show one or the other. Always check the full label and its storage instructions, because poor storage can affect food before the printed date.

## Best before vs use by: the key difference

A **use-by** date is a safety deadline for food that can become unsafe to eat quickly. A **best-before** date tells you when food may begin to lose quality, such as its flavour or texture. It is not a general instruction to treat both dates in the same way.

The [UK government guide to best-before and use-by dates](https://www.gov.uk/understanding-food-labelling/best-before-and-use-by-dates) explains which foods use these dates and what the label means. The guidance is more important than a rule of thumb or advice from someone who has not seen the product and its storage history.

### What a use-by date means

Use-by dates appear on foods that can go off quickly. The government gives meat products and ready-to-eat salads as examples. The date tells you when the food is no longer considered safe to eat under the label's conditions.

Do not eat food after its use-by date, even if it looks and smells normal. Your senses cannot reliably establish whether it is safe. If the date is approaching, plan when to eat it or check the official instructions for cooking or freezing it before the deadline.

### What a best-before date means

Best-before dates are about quality. They can appear on foods such as dried pasta and rice, tinned food, frozen foods and cheese. After the date, some products may not taste or feel as good, but the date alone does not mean they have automatically become unsafe.

For food with a best-before date, follow the package's storage instructions. When the date has passed, check the food carefully for visible changes, such as mould, and use your judgement about quality. This approach is not a substitute for checking a use-by date: never use smell or taste to overrule one.

## How storage changes the advice

A printed date assumes you have stored the product as directed. The label might tell you to keep it chilled, use it within a certain time after opening, or store it in a particular place. If you have not followed those instructions, the food could become unsafe or lose quality sooner.

The government guidance says food that needs refrigeration should be kept at **5°C or below**, unless its label gives different instructions. Check your fridge temperature rather than guessing; settings can vary between appliances. Keep food in its original packaging when that is how its instructions are displayed, and read the full label before opening it.

Opening a packet can change how long the food keeps. Look for an instruction such as “once opened, consume within…” and note when you opened it. A best-before or use-by date does not cancel a separate after-opening instruction.

## Can you cook or freeze food before a use-by date?

The government says you can eat food after its use-by date if it was **cooked or frozen on or before the use-by date**. It also gives steps for food cooked before that date: cool it, keep it in the fridge, and eat it within 48 hours or freeze it. Follow the food's own label and the [official instructions on chilling, freezing and defrosting](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/how-to-chill-freeze-and-defrost-food-safely).

If freezing food, do it by the use-by date and label the item so you know what it is and when it went into the freezer. The government guidance says to keep the freezer at **−18°C** and to follow the product's defrosting instructions. Once food has been defrosted, the guidance says to use it within 24 hours. Do not rely on these steps if the food may already have been stored unsafely.

Cooking or freezing does not make food safe if it has already passed its use-by date. Planning ahead is the useful part: check dates while storing groceries and decide whether something needs to be cooked or frozen in time.

## A simple way to check a food label

1. **Find the date wording.** Check whether the package says “use by” or “best before”; they are not interchangeable.
2. **Read the storage instructions.** Look for refrigeration requirements and any separate instruction for after opening.
3. **Check how it has been stored.** If the product needed chilling, consider whether it stayed at the recommended temperature.
4. **For a use-by date, do not eat it after the date.** If you want to keep it longer, check whether you can cook or freeze it before the date.
5. **For a best-before date, assess quality.** The date is not itself a safety verdict; check the food and packaging carefully.
6. **If a label is unclear, stop and check.** Use current official guidance or contact the food business rather than guessing.

## Common mistakes to avoid

**Treating both dates as a safety deadline:** a best-before date describes quality, while a use-by date is the safety one.

**Using smell to check food after its use-by date:** food may appear normal and still not be safe. Follow the date.

**Ignoring storage or after-opening instructions:** a printed date is not a guarantee if the food has been kept incorrectly.

**Assuming freezing can happen whenever you like:** check the official advice and freeze food before its use-by date.

**Relying on generic advice instead of the packet:** products and instructions differ. Read the label for the food you actually have.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is food safe to eat after its best-before date?

The best-before date is about quality, so passing it does not automatically mean the food is unsafe. Check the food and its storage history carefully, and follow any package instructions.

### Can I eat food after its use-by date if it smells fine?

No. The UK government says not to eat food after its use-by date, even if it looks and smells normal. Smell is not a reliable safety test.

### Can I freeze food on its use-by date?

The government says food can be frozen on or before its use-by date. Check the label and current official freezing and defrosting guidance for the particular food.

### Where can I check official UK food-date guidance?

Read the [UK government guide to food dates](https://www.gov.uk/understanding-food-labelling/best-before-and-use-by-dates). Use the product label for its own storage and after-opening instructions.

## The takeaway

Remember one distinction: **use by is about safety; best before is about quality**. Then check the storage and opening instructions on the product. For current details, use the [UK government guidance on food date labels](https://www.gov.uk/understanding-food-labelling/best-before-and-use-by-dates) rather than treating a general tip as a guarantee.
