Getting more active does not have to mean joining a gym or training for a race. Walking briskly, cycling, dancing and some everyday activities can all count. The right plan depends on your health, routine and starting point, so use NHS guidance rather than comparing yourself with a professional athlete or a viral fitness challenge.

This article summarises the NHS recommendations for **adults aged 19 to 64**. It is general information, not a personal exercise plan or medical advice. Read the current [NHS physical activity guidelines for adults](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-for-adults-aged-19-to-64/) and ask a health professional if you are unsure what is suitable for you.

## What activity do adults need each week?

The NHS says adults should do some type of physical activity every day. It recommends aiming for at least **150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity** a week, or **75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity**, or a combination. It also recommends muscle-strengthening activities that work all major muscle groups on at least **two days a week**.

Those are weekly targets, not a requirement to do one long session. The NHS says activity can be spread across four to five days a week or done every day. Choose a pattern that works with your schedule and ability, and build up gradually if you have been inactive.

The recommendations in this guide are specifically for adults aged 19 to 64. Children, teenagers and older adults have guidance for their age groups. Pregnancy, disability or a health condition may also affect what is appropriate; check the relevant NHS advice rather than applying a general adult plan without adjustment.

## What counts as moderate activity?

Moderate activity makes your heart beat faster, makes you breathe more quickly and can make you feel warmer. The NHS gives a practical talking test: if you can still talk but not sing, the activity may be moderate.

Examples listed by the NHS include brisk walking, water aerobics, cycling, dancing, doubles tennis, hiking and pushing a lawn mower. These examples show that activity can fit different routines and abilities. You do not have to play a competitive sport to count movement toward your weekly target.

## What counts as vigorous activity?

During vigorous activity, you breathe hard and fast; you may not be able to say more than a few words without pausing for breath. NHS examples include running, swimming, fast cycling or cycling uphill, football, rugby, netball, skipping and aerobics.

The NHS says 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week can give similar health benefits to 150 minutes of moderate activity. That does not mean every person should choose vigorous exercise. If it feels too intense, try a more moderate option and seek advice if you have a medical condition or concern.

Short, very vigorous bursts such as high-intensity interval training are another activity type described by the NHS. A trend or workout video is not automatically suitable for everyone. Start at a level you can manage and stop if you feel unwell.

## Why include strength activities?

The weekly aerobic activity target is not the whole recommendation. The NHS also advises muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days each week. Examples include weights, resistance bands, yoga, Pilates, body-weight movements, heavy gardening, and lifting or carrying children.

The NHS says strength exercises should work all the major muscle groups. You can do them on the same days as aerobic activity or on different days, depending on what suits you. If you have not tried strength exercise before, choose a manageable activity and learn how to do it safely rather than copying a routine that uses more weight or repetitions than you can control.

## How to start when your routine is busy

**Choose something practical.** A walk, a bike ride or an activity you already enjoy may be easier to repeat than an ambitious new routine.

**Break up long periods of sitting.** The NHS recommends reducing time spent sitting or lying down and breaking up long periods without movement with some activity.

**Add time gradually.** If you are new to exercise, short sessions can help establish a routine. Increase duration or intensity at a pace that feels appropriate rather than trying to reach the weekly target in one go.

**Make it convenient.** Pick days and times that fit around work, care responsibilities and rest. A plan you can maintain is more useful than one that disrupts your sleep or leaves no room for recovery.

**Keep it flexible.** Weather, illness, travel and changes in schedule happen. If you miss a session, return to your routine when you can; there is no need to compensate with a workout that feels unsafe.

## When to ask for medical advice

The NHS advises speaking to a GP before starting if you have not exercised for some time, or if you have medical conditions or concerns. It also says to make sure your activity and its intensity are appropriate for your fitness.

If you experience worrying symptoms during activity, stop and seek appropriate medical help. This guide cannot assess personal symptoms or replace advice from a clinician who knows your situation. Use current NHS guidance, especially if you are managing an illness, recovering from an injury, pregnant or adapting exercise for a disability.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many minutes of exercise should adults aim for each week?

For adults aged 19 to 64, the NHS recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, or a combination, plus strength activities on at least two days.

### Does walking count as physical activity?

Yes. The NHS lists brisk walking as an example of moderate-intensity activity. Whether a walk is moderate depends on its pace and how it affects your breathing and heart rate.

### Can I do my weekly activity in short sessions?

Activity can be spread over several days. Choose a routine that suits your circumstances and abilities, and consult current NHS guidance or a health professional if you are unsure where to start.

### Should I speak to a GP before starting exercise?

The NHS advises speaking to a GP if you have not exercised for some time, or if you have medical conditions or concerns. Follow advice that is specific to your health.

## Use the guidance that fits you

There is no single sport or schedule that works for everyone. The NHS adult guidelines offer a starting point: include regular movement, build up to a suitable level, and add strength activity if appropriate. For more detail, age groups and health circumstances, read the [NHS physical activity guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-for-adults-aged-19-to-64/).
